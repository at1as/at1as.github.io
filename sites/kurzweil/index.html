Where was Ray Kurzweil right?

Kurzweil put deadlines on wireless networks, wearable computers and self-driving cars. We checked 84 of his forecasts against what happened.

Networks and medical tools fare better in this collection than robotics and transport. Of 16 computing forecasts, 10 came true, 5 by the deadline.

How the 13 categories are defined

Computing hardware

Claims about computing capacity, size and cost.

Networks & communications

Transport and availability of information, not language understanding.

AI & software

Software abilities. Tag commercial adoption separately.

Interfaces & virtual worlds

Distinguish headsets, retinal displays, contact lenses and direct neural interfaces.

Biology & medicine

Separate computational research milestones from human clinical outcomes.

Longevity

Keep population, age group, access and life-expectancy definition explicit.

Neuroscience & human augmentation

Brain hardware, brain understanding and cognition augmentation.

Robotics & transportation

Distinguish prototype, commercial service and widespread use.

Manufacturing & materials

Medical nanobots: primary category Biology & medicine; nanotech as a tag.

Energy & environment

Preserve energy versus electricity, capacity versus generation.

Economy & society

Track geography and population; separate causal claims from observed events.

Media, education & culture

Do not confuse availability with dominant use.

Security & warfare

Distinguish device existence from prevalence or displacement of personnel.

What counts as a hit?

We judge each claim against its deadline and intended scale. A working product can satisfy a capability forecast; a prediction of widespread use also needs evidence of adoption.

Consumer preference and regulation can limit use even when a technology works. We judge those cases individually and show alternative readings where the result is ambiguous.

CorrectTarget: 2009

Wireless peripherals

Wireless keyboards and mice were in ordinary use by 2009. He was right about the technology, but less accurate about consumer preference: wired devices remained in use.

Read the evidence
LateTarget: 2009

Brain-scale supercomputing

Tianhe-2 exceeded the 20-petaflop benchmark in 2013, four years after the deadline. This measures computing performance, not a simulation of the human brain.

Read the evidence
PartialTarget: 2019

Robots in every home

Robot vacuums handle a specific chore. The forecast called for robots in nearly every household, doing a broad range of chores.

What this collection can tell us

It compares 84 distinct forecast versions. Repeated claims count once; materially different deadlines or thresholds stay separate. 68 are due, 14 are future, and 2 lack a usable deadline.

The selection is incomplete and uneven across fields. Most book claims were checked through reprints or retrospectives; original editions still need checking. The research and judgments are AI-assisted and have not had independent human review.

Outcomes are assessed through October 15, 2026. The sources and downloads include all 88 original intake records and earlier assessments.
