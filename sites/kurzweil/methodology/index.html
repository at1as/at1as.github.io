HOW WE JUDGE

How we judge a prediction

We check each claim against its original deadline and stated scale. Every record lays out what would count as success, links to the evidence, and shows how stricter or more generous readings change the result.

What counts as success?

We read each prediction in context, preserving its cost, capability, adoption, geography, mechanism, and deadline requirements. A trend claim doesn’t require universal adoption, and a working prototype doesn’t satisfy a claim about everyday use.

Wireless keyboards and mice were practical and widely used by 2009. Kurzweil called the technology right, even though wired peripherals didn’t disappear. The Balanced reading credits that shift toward wireless; the Literal reading tests how completely cables vanished. The household robot forecast set a much higher bar: robots in nearly every home, handling a wide range of chores.

Preference, cost, and regulation often slow adoption long after a technology works. We weigh those cases individually and show three readings—Literal, Balanced, and Charitable—so you can see exactly where reasonable interpretations diverge. A record is marked Disputed whenever those readings produce different verdicts.

Literal, Balanced, and Charitable readings

Every due prediction is assessed under all three readings. Literal enforces the stated mechanism, form factor, scope, and adoption bar closely. Balanced (our default) uses a fair, common-sense reading in context. Charitable credits reasonable functional substitutes—like cloud storage in place of home servers—without rewriting failed technologies into unrelated hits.

Five outcomes

On time

Fully realized by its deadline under the selected reading.

Late

Fully realized after the deadline. Counts as realized, but not on time.

Partial

Meaningful progress happened, but the core claim hasn’t been fully met yet. Partial receives no fractional credit.

Missed

The deadline passed and the claim went unfulfilled as of our review cutoff. That isn’t a claim it can never happen later.

Pending

The deadline is still ahead. We track current progress, but keep upcoming bets out of the scorecard rates.

Every record tracks both the outcome at its original deadline and its status as of October 15, 2026.

Unreviewed, unscorable, and excluded records

Unreviewed is an intake state, not a failure. Unscorable means no clear test or usable deadline exists. Excluded marks a duplicate or misattributed claim. None of these enters the rates.

Two undated predictions still show outcome verdicts in the full list, but stay out of deadline-based rates.

Late means it happened

Passing a deadline without fulfillment doesn’t make a prediction “late”—it makes it missed or partial. Earning a Late verdict requires evidence that the prediction actually came true after its target year.

The supercomputing forecast targeted 2009 and crossed 20 petaflops on sustained LINPACK in 2013—four years late. The chess forecast was fulfilled in 1997 under both of its cited 1998 and 2000 deadlines, leaving its outcome clear even though the exact edition date varies.

“Documented by 2008” means our earliest cited evidence dates to 2008, not that 2008 was necessarily the first year it happened. Whole-year deadlines close on December 31; at our October 15, 2026 cutoff, 2026 deadlines remain open. Approximate windows such as “mid-2020s” or “early 2030s” close at the end of the window (2026 and 2033).

The limits of this collection

This is a curated collection of 81 predictions, not a complete census of every forecast Ray Kurzweil has ever made. Fields vary in sample size—a one-prediction category can’t tell you much on its own—and older targets have had longer to arrive late than 2019 ones.

The research, claim paraphrases, and editorial verdicts are AI-assisted and have not undergone independent human peer review. Most book claims were checked through Kurzweil’s retrospectives, reprints, or archived editions rather than page-by-page collation against first-edition print copies. A complete lifetime audit would require systematically extracting all 147 predictions for 2009 alongside his later books and interviews.

Every original intake row (88 leads) and versioned release (v0.1 through v0.4) is preserved for inspection.
